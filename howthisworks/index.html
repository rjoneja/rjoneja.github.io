How Ordo works

A plain-English tour of what happens behind the scenes when you play. No technical knowledge needed.

Three pieces

Ordo is made of just three things: the game itself, your phone, and a small scorekeeper that runs the leaderboard.

The whole game, including every question, is one file of about 1 MB, smaller than a typical phone photo. It's like a booklet handed to you at the door: once you have it, you don't need to go back for more. That's why Ordo is quick, and why a run carries on even if your signal drops halfway through.

What happens when you play

  1. Your phone fetches the gameYour browser then keeps a copy for a while, so opening Ordo again is near-instant. It picks up a newer version when there is one.
  2. It works out today's puzzle numberSimply the number of days since Ordo began on 21 October 2026. Monday 26 October is puzzle 6.
  3. It builds today's sets from that numberEvery phone gets exactly the same sets, in the same order. More on how below.
  4. You play, entirely on your phoneThe clock, the dragging, the checking: nothing is sent anywhere while you play.
  5. Your score goes to the scorekeeperJust the number, plus a random code that stands in for "this phone". No name unless you add one.
  6. It replies with your rank"3rd today". Make the top 5 and you're asked for a name for the leaderboard.

The same puzzle for everyone, without sending it

Nobody emails out today's questions. Instead, every phone already has all the questions, and follows the same recipe to pick from them.

Think of a pack of cards and a written rule: "shuffle exactly like this, using the number 6." Anyone with the same pack and the same rule ends up with the same order. Tomorrow the number is 7, so the order is different, but still the same for everyone.

Free play uses the same recipe with a random number, so every free play run is different and never affects the daily puzzle. You can play it with the clock, with a relaxed clock, or with no clock at all.

Where the questions come from

Ordo carries a bank of about 165 kinds of question ("Which is heaviest?", "Which country is biggest?"), built from 1,800 ready-made sets and around 14,000 names. The figures come from public reference data: country statistics, the periodic table, chart histories, record books, and everyday facts like how hot an oven gets.

Difficulty comes mostly from how close the answers are, not from obscure facts. Early on, Ordo picks things far apart (a mouse, a horse, a whale). Later it picks things nearer each other (a horse, a cow, a giraffe), which takes more thought.

How a run gets harder

The clock starts generous and quickly settles at 7 seconds. After that, every 5 sets adds one more thing to sort. Much later the clock tightens again, so every run ends eventually.

What's kept where

📱 On your phone only

  • Your streak and best scores
  • Your settings (light or dark, relaxed clock)
  • The name you chose for the leaderboard
  • A random code meaning "this phone"

📒 Sent to the scorekeeper

  • Your daily score, with that random code
  • Your leaderboard name, only if you add one
  • Anonymous counts: visits, shares, how long each set took, type of device, screen size, rough town

🚫 Never collected

  • Your real name, email or phone number
  • Your exact location (no GPS)
  • Your contacts or photos
  • Cookies or adverts

Because everything personal lives on your phone, clearing your browser's data resets your streak. That's the trade-off for not needing an account.

If it suddenly gets popular

Both outside services, GitHub and Cloudflare, keep copies of Ordo on computers in many cities around the world, so a player in Leeds and a player in Sydney are each served from somewhere nearby.

The leaderboard uses a trick like a shop's "now serving" board. Instead of recounting every score for every visitor, the scorekeeper refreshes the board every 20 seconds and shows everyone that copy, always with your own score added. Ten people or ten thousand, the work stays roughly the same.

Running costs: the game hosting is free, and the scorekeeper is free up to about 100,000 requests a day. Beyond that it's about $5 a month. The only fixed cost is the yearly fee for the ordogame.com name.

Ordo Kids' voice

Emma, the voice in Ordo Kids, is an AI voice that runs on an ordinary computer rather than a paid online service. Every sentence the game can say, over 500 of them, was recorded once and joined into a single sound file of about 15 minutes.

When the game needs a line, it skips straight to the right moment in that file, like jumping to a track on a CD. Words the voice tended to get wrong (like "live" in "lives longest") are spelled out by sound, so Emma says them properly.

Ordo Kids keeps even less than the main game: no lasting code for the device and no town, in line with the UK's rules for apps used by children.

Checking it works

Before any change goes live, about 300 automatic checks run. Robot "players" open the game on pretend phones of different sizes, play whole runs, drag, tap and swipe, switch to dark mode, lose their internet connection, and open two copies at once. Any failure stops the release.

Learning from play

For every set played, the game counts (anonymously) whether it was solved and how long it took. Once there are a few thousand of these, a widely used statistics method called XGBoost looks for patterns. It combines lots of simple yes-or-no rules ("are the answers close?", "are there 5 items?", "is it about films?") into a good guess at how hard a set really is.

The aim is simple: every day should feel about as hard as the last, and climb smoothly instead of in jumps.
